Muse Can Now Call Businesses for You If You Pay: What the November 12 Rollout Actually Opens Up

Muse can now place calls to US businesses on your behalf, but only if you pay for Muse. TechCrunch reported the rollout on November 12, 2026: after a September test with a select number of early adopters who asked Muse for access, calling opened that day to all paid US subscribers. Free users are not the audience for this feature. TechCrunch states the paid-only limit plainly, in words to the effect of for the time being, and attributes that limit to Meta itself.

By Marvin Jones

Last checked against the live product: November 13, 2026.

This is an expansion, not a launch

The date that matters for anyone searching this week is November 12, but the feature is older than that date. Meta tested calling in September. The test group was small and self-selected: early adopters who asked Muse for access got to try it. What changed on November 12 is the size of the door, not the existence of the feature. Before that day, you needed to be picked from a pool of people who requested it. After that day, a paid US subscription is the ticket.

That distinction changes what a reader should expect. A feature that has been in a limited test since September has had two months of real calls behind it, and the problems found in that period are part of the record in the next section. It also means earlier how-it-works reporting from September and October describes the test version. This post uses that older reporting only where it is labeled with its date, and it does not carry any test-era mechanic forward as a fact about the paid rollout unless a November 12 or November 13 source confirms it. None did for the mechanics readers most want to know: transcripts, listening in, or stopping a call in progress.

One more boundary sits inside the confirmed row. The rollout is for paid US subscribers calling US businesses. No source checked on November 12 or November 13 extends it to free accounts, to subscribers outside the US, or to calls to businesses outside the US.

The hang-up problem is in the launch report itself

TechCrunch does not bury the weak point. Its November 12 report states that many businesses and receptionists hang up on calls from AI agents once they identify that the caller is not human. That is the adverse fact for this whole feature, and it comes from the same article that carries the rollout, not from a critic writing later.

The same report gives the hang-up problem a history inside Muse itself. TechCrunch notes that the problem may have played a role in Meta earlier testing human contractors placing calls on Muse's behalf, a test it says Meta rolled back after backlash, citing Reuters reporting. Read those two facts together and the November 12 rollout looks like a second attempt at a hard problem: an AI caller that businesses may refuse to talk to, after a human-caller workaround that users and observers rejected.

No source checked for this post publishes a success rate for Muse calls, a completion rate by business type, or any figure for how often a November call ended in a hang-up. Explainx, in a November 12 analysis read for this post, lists the same absences from the other side: no published per-call limits, no public allowlist of supported businesses, no published success metrics, and no details about recording or retention in the November 12 material. Until Meta publishes numbers of its own, any claim that Muse calling usually works, or usually fails, is a guess with a date missing from it.

Field note

The first call worth handing Muse is a low-stakes one where a hang-up costs you nothing: a restaurant with no online reservations, where the worst outcome is that you call back yourself. A cable or insurance call, where the result changes a bill, is the wrong first test while caller identification, transcripts for the paid rollout, and call limits are all unpublished. You cannot audit a negotiation you have no record of, and no November source checked here confirms what record a paid subscriber gets after a call.

What you pay, and what that payment is stated to buy

Hold the figures to the one source that printed them in full. TechCrunch, November 12, 2026, cites the paid plans at $20 per month for 500 million Muse tokens per week, and $100 per month for 3 billion Muse tokens per week. Those are the Power and Maximum prices this site has carried all November, free with a usage limit below them, and the calling rollout named no new tier and no calling add-on price.

What the article does not do is price the call itself. There is no per-call charge stated, no per-minute figure, and no statement of how a phone call lasting through a long hold queue counts against a weekly token allowance. A call that waits on hold is the exact use Wang's announcement sells, holding time being the chore being handed off, and the meter question for that use is unanswered in every source opened for this post. PCMag, in a November 13 rewrite read for this post, adds one mechanic and nothing on price: Muse will use an AI-generated voice on the calls, per PCMag. The Decoder and Gadgets360, both November 13 and both read for this post, are rewrites of the same Wang and TechCrunch material, with competitor context and no new calling facts.

Caution

Do not read a paid subscription as a promise that a specific business will take the call. The subscription buys access to the feature, on the November 12 record. It does not buy a published list of businesses that accept AI calls, a stated success rate, or a stated remedy when a receptionist hangs up. If a business matters to you, the check that counts is still the ordinary one: call it once yourself and ask whether it will work with an AI caller on your behalf.

The email item in the same report, kept separate

TechCrunch's November 12 article carries a second Wang item, and this post keeps it in its own paragraph because it runs in the opposite direction on price. Wang said the option to create a Muse email address will soon roll out to free users. No date was given, in that article or anywhere else checked on November 13.

The dated record under that promise is narrow. Wang posted on November 10, 2026 that Muse has its own email address, that you can forward it material or add it to a thread, and that it was out then to paid subscribers in the US and Canada. That post was read directly for this post. So the sequence is: email address to paid subscribers on November 10, a promise of free access with the word soon on November 12, and no free-tier date as of November 13. When a date appears, it will be its own news item. It does not make calling free, and it does not soften the paid-only line for calling, which TechCrunch attributes to Meta for the time being.

What did not change on November 12

The country list did not change. Muse is available in the US, Canada, Japan, Singapore, and Mexico on this site's dated record, and calling is stated for US subscribers and US businesses only, which is a smaller map than the app itself.

Three standing items hold flat, because calling coverage keeps pulling them in. Spaces sharing is still not live: Meta described it on November 6 as exploring ways to share. Expedia in Muse is still hotels only on the Skift record of November 9, with flights not verified. The UK is still reported as coming within weeks, with no date.

India did gain a timing window in separate reporting this week, and it is still not a launch. India remains priced and waitlisted, at 1,799 rupees a month for Power and 8,999 rupees a month for Maximum per The Hindu of November 9. On November 12, The Economic Times and Livemint both headlined an interview with Meta India head Arun Srinivas as putting Muse in India in early 2027, with Moneycontrol and YourStory carrying the same claim and the Times of India repeating it on November 13. That reporting is snippet-level only for this post: no article body was read in full, no Meta primary was found, and no exact date was given. It is the first timing window named for India on this site's record, at that weight, and it does not change the waitlisted status today.

No Meta primary beyond Wang's November 12 post was found for calling. DuckDuckGo checks on November 13 found no Meta blog post, newsroom post, or help page about calling published on November 12 or November 13, and no in-app sighting from a paid account was reported in any source opened. One secondary source, TechMyMoney on November 13, reports that the official Muse account on X also announced the feature as available now for paid US subscribers. That account post was not opened directly for this post, so it is carried here as secondary reporting, at that weight.

Next: the November 20 recheck, and one calling question

The reader question for this post is the one the examples list cannot answer for you: what business would you actually let Muse call for you first: cable, DMV, insurance, or a restaurant? The restaurant answer tests whether the call connects and completes. The cable and insurance answers test whether you trust an AI caller with money on the line, while the terms of that trust, identification, limits, and records, are still unpublished. Answers that name the business type and what you would need to see after the call, a transcript, a summary, a confirmation number, will shape the November 20 recheck more than another rollout post will.

Quick answers

Can Muse call a business for me?

Yes, if you are a paid subscriber in the US. TechCrunch reported on November 12, 2026 that Muse can place calls to US businesses on behalf of users, after a September test with a select number of early adopters who asked for access.

Is Muse calling free?

No. The feature is only available to paid subscribers for the time being, per TechCrunch on November 12, 2026, attributing the limit to Meta. Paid plans are cited in that report at $20 per month for 500 million tokens per week and $100 per month for 3 billion tokens per week.

Is calling a new Muse feature?

No. Meta tested it in September 2026 with early adopters who asked for access. November 12 is the date it opened to all paid US subscribers.

What kinds of businesses can Muse call?

The examples in TechCrunch, November 12, 2026, are a cable company, the DMV, and a restaurant that does not offer online reservations. Insurance appears in Alexandr Wang's own list, as rendered by TechMyMoney on November 13. No public allowlist of supported businesses was found in any source checked on November 13.

What does a Muse call cost per call, and how long can it last?

Not stated. No per-call price, duration limit, or call cap was found in any source checked on November 12 or November 13, 2026. Explainx, on November 12, reached the same absence finding: no published per-call limits in the November 12 material.

How does Muse identify itself on a call, and what shows on caller ID?

Not stated. No source checked on November 12 or November 13 says what the person who answers hears, whether Muse identifies itself as an AI, or what number appears on caller ID. PCMag, on November 13, reports only that Muse will use an AI-generated voice.

Will the business just hang up?

Some will, on the launch report itself. TechCrunch, November 12, 2026, states that many businesses and receptionists hang up on calls from AI agents once they identify the caller is not human. No success rate for Muse calls has been published in any source checked.

When do free users get anything from the November 12 news?

The one free-tier item is a promise with no date. Wang said the Muse email address option will soon roll out to free users, per TechCrunch on November 12. The email feature itself launched to paid subscribers in the US and Canada, per Wang's post of November 10, 2026.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: Who announced the November 12 rollout, and where?

A: Alexandr Wang, Meta chief AI officer, in a post on November 12, 2026, as reported by TechCrunch the same day, in a piece by Sarah Perez published at 2:22 PM PST. No separate Meta blog post was linked in that article, and DuckDuckGo checks on November 13 found no Meta blog, newsroom, or help page about calling published on November 12 or November 13.

Q: What is the difference between the September test and the November rollout?

A: Access. In September, a select number of early adopters could try calling after asking Muse for access. On November 12, calling opened to all paid US subscribers. The feature itself, and its examples, are the same line of work in both rounds, on the sources checked.

Q: Did Meta publish how calling works inside the app?

A: Not in any source found for this post. No in-app sighting, walkthrough, or help page for the paid rollout was reported in the sources opened on November 12 and November 13. TechMyMoney, a secondary source on November 13, reports an announcement post from the official Muse account on X, which was not opened directly for this post.

Q: Has Muse used human callers before?

A: That is what TechCrunch reports about an earlier test. Its November 12 article notes that the hang-up problem may have played a role in Meta testing human contractors placing calls on Muse's behalf, and that Meta rolled back that test after facing backlash, citing Reuters. That is test history from a secondary account of Reuters reporting, and this post carries it at that weight.

Q: Do I get a transcript or summary after Muse calls for me?

A: Not confirmed for the paid rollout. Reporting from the September and October test period, cited in the checks for this post, described a transcript and a summary after a call. No November 12 or November 13 source checked confirms that mechanic for the paid US rollout, so this post does not present it as a current feature.

Q: How does Muse calling compare with Gemini or other rivals?
