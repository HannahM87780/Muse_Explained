Muse's Chrome Extension Is Live With 200,000 Users Already: What It Can See in Your Browser, What It Cannot, and How to Take Control Back

The Chrome Web Store listing for Muse from Meta shows 200,000 users, a 4.6 rating, and version 2.1.9 updated November 19, 2026. Meta's own help page says Muse browses in your personal Chrome, in its own tab group, on sites you are already logged into, and cannot see your saved passwords, your browsing history, or your Chrome settings. No source checked on November 20 states a general region list, and no CNET or Verge safety verdict on the extension was found.

By Marvin Jones

Last checked against the live product: November 20, 2026.

Verdict first: install it only after you decide which logged-in sites Muse may use. Meta's help page says Muse can use any site you are already logged into, and tells you to log out of the accounts you want kept out before you connect the extension. That one rule, not the 200,000-user count, is the decision in this post. Start with a low-cost public page, check that the approval prompt appears, and read the activity log after. This site rechecks the two open items below, a general region list and an independent safety test, on November 27, 2026.

How the extension works, on Meta's own pages

Open Muse in Chrome's side panel without leaving the page you are on. The listing gives two ways in: press Alt+Shift+C, or click the Muse icon in the toolbar. The extension requires Chrome 137 or later, and the listing says browser use in Chrome works best with a Muse account.

Ask Muse to do the browsing and it takes action in your personal Chrome, not in a separate browser of its own. That sentence is the whole design, and it cuts both ways. Muse opens its own tabs in a group, so you can watch it work, and Meta's help page says Muse can take actions only in those tabs. Work outside that group is outside its reach, on Meta's account. Work inside that group runs with your Chrome, your sessions, and your logged-in state.

The listing's own task list is ordinary busywork: filling out forms, comparing prices, following up on refunds, booking trips. One line carries the only region statement in the record. For paid subscribers in the U.S., Muse can also handle shopping, travel bookings, and phone calls. Read that line at its exact width. It assigns the shopping, booking, and calling work to paid subscribers in the United States. It does not say the extension itself is United States only, and no source checked on November 20 says that either.

What Muse can use in your browser

The widest fact in this record is one sentence on Meta's help page. Muse can use any website that you are already logged into, without asking for your login details again.

That is the feature. It is also the exposure. A bank tab, a work inbox, a medical portal, and a shopping account are all the same kind of object to this rule: a site where your browser is already signed in. Meta's help page gives the matching instruction in plain terms. To keep Muse out of accounts you do not want it to use, log out of them before you connect the extension. The logout has to happen first, on the user's side. No setting in the sources checked offers a per-site block list instead.

Meta's help page adds two smaller mechanics. Credentials sit in a Secure Credentials Store, and Muse can use them without the AI model seeing information like passwords. Muse may also accept essential cookies on third-party websites while it browses. Both points come from the help page. Neither appears in the Web Store overview, and no source checked explains which sites trigger the cookie point.

Field note

The Web Store privacy page and the Web Store overview do not say the same thing in the same words, and a careful reader should hold both. The overview says Muse cannot access your saved passwords, browser history, or Chrome settings. The privacy page, read in full on November 20, lists the data the extension handles, and that list includes web history and authentication information among its nine categories: personally identifiable information, health information, financial and payment information, authentication information, personal communications, location, web history, user activity, and website content. A Chrome Web Store privacy disclosure is a broad handling declaration, not a claim that Muse reads your history file. Still, the two pages sit side by side on the same listing, and Meta has not, in any source checked, explained how the cannot-access line and the handles-web-history line fit together. That gap is printed here as a gap, not smoothed over.

What Muse cannot see, as Meta states it

Three items, stated the same way on the listing and on the help page: your saved passwords, your browsing history, and your Chrome settings. A fourth protection covers the page in front of Muse. Passwords on a webpage are removed from the text before Muse reads it, per the help page.

Those are Meta's statements about its own product. No independent test of the cannot-see list was found on November 20. CNET and The Verge, the two outlets this site checked first for a tested verdict, had published no extension safety article dated November 19 or 20 in the searches run that day. Digital Trends, read in full, describes the launch and repeats Meta's warnings. It gives no tested verdict of its own. Until an outside test exists, the cannot-see list stands in this record as a vendor claim, clearly labelled, not as a checked fact about every site and every version.

The controls, and how to take the browser back

The listing groups its controls under two headings, and the wording is specific enough to check against your own install.

Approve before action. The listing says you can approve actions before they happen, review what Muse sends and spends, and step in at any time. If Muse is using a service on your behalf, it will ask you before it does anything that may cost you money. The help page adds where the default lives: approval defaults are managed by the Web access default setting, in Permissions under Settings.

Activity log and revocation. The listing says Muse keeps a full activity log so you can review every action it takes, and that you can revoke any connector at any time.

Taking control back takes one of three moves, per the help page: click on the page, press Esc, or move the tab out of the Muse group of tabs. Moving the tab is the most final of the three, because the group is the boundary Meta itself states. A tab outside the group is a tab Muse cannot act in.

Caution

Meta warns, as Digital Trends reported it on November 19, that Muse may take unexpected actions in the browser. The help page adds a second cost that is easier to miss. Muse's activity may show up in your Google account and browsing history, and may affect the ads, recommendations, and content you see on and off Meta Company Products. A price comparison Muse runs for you on Tuesday can shape the ads served to you on Friday, on Meta's own statement. Readers who install the extension for one private task should weigh that sentence before the task, not after.

The privacy declarations on the listing

Chrome requires developers to declare how they handle data, and Meta's declarations on this listing are three negatives. User data is not sold to third parties outside the approved use cases. It is not used or transferred for purposes unrelated to the item's core functionality. It is not used or transferred to determine creditworthiness or for lending purposes.

Declarations are not audits. They are the developer's own statements, filed on the store page, and Chrome's listing presents them as such. They belong in the record because a reader deciding whether to install is reading exactly this page. They do not answer the web history question raised in the field note above, and this post does not use them to close it.

What a first session should look like

Start with a site where a mistake costs little. A price comparison on a public store page uses the tab group, the browsing, and the reporting back, and it touches no account you care about. Watch whether the approval prompt appears before Muse acts, and read the activity log afterward while the session is still fresh in your mind. The log is only useful as a control if you learn its shape on a task you already understand.

Log out of the accounts you do not want touched before that first session, not after. Meta's own instruction puts the logout before connecting the extension. Then, if the work goes well, sign back into one account at a time as a task needs it. That order is slower. It is also the only order the help page describes.

Records this post does not change

Three standing records sit near this launch and none of them moves. Consumer Muse pricing is still free, with Power at $20 a month and Maximum at $100 a month. The paid subscriber line on the extension listing fits inside that record and adds no new tier or price. Spaces sharing is still not live: Meta described it on November 6 as exploring ways to share, and Digital Trends noting that custom Spaces can be built in the Muse app is a building fact, not a sharing launch. Try On, announced November 18, still has no Meta detail post on region, price, platform, or steps. An extension launch answers none of those rows.

One separate feature was confirmed on November 20 and belongs in the dated record, not in the Chrome record. Meta's Help Center page titled About Muse email addresses confirms that Muse can have its own @muse.ai address, set up in the Muse app, separate from your personal email, with approval asked before Muse sends. The page was read in full on November 20. Its stated update time is about a week earlier, so November 20 is when the coverage surfaced, not a proven launch date, and no rollout scope is confirmed in that read. The email confirmation changes no permission, price, or control stated for the Chrome extension.

Quick answers

What is the Muse Chrome extension?

An extension called Muse from Meta on the Chrome Web Store. It puts Muse in Chrome's side panel, and Muse browses in your personal Chrome, opening its own tabs in a group and acting only inside those tabs, per Meta's help page.

When did it launch?

TestingCatalog spotted the Web Store listing on November 13, 2026, per Digital Trends. Alexandr Wang announced it on November 20, 2026, in a post Digital Trends describes as belated. The listing itself was last updated November 19, 2026, to version 2.1.9.

How many people use it already?

The Web Store listing showed 200,000 users on November 20, 2026, with a 4.6 star rating from 237 ratings. Wang's announcement used the same 200,000 figure, as Digital Trends reported it.

What can Muse not see in Chrome?

Saved passwords, browsing history, and Chrome settings, per both the Web Store listing and Meta's help page. Passwords on a webpage are removed from the text before Muse reads the page, per the help page. No independent test of this list was found on November 20, 2026.

Which sites can Muse use?

Any website you are already logged into, without asking for your login details again, per Meta's help page. To keep Muse out of an account, log out of it before connecting the extension.

Where is the extension available?

No general region list is stated on the Web Store listing or in any source checked on November 20, 2026. The only region wording in the record is that paid subscribers in the U.S. can have Muse handle shopping, travel bookings, and phone calls.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: How do I open Muse in Chrome?

A: Press Alt+Shift+C, or click the Muse icon in the toolbar, per the Web Store listing. The extension requires Chrome 137 or later. Meta's help page gives the setup route as asking Muse in Chat to download the Chrome Extension, then signing in to your Muse account.

Q: How do I stop Muse while it is browsing?

A: Click on the page, press Esc, or move the tab out of the Muse group of tabs, per Meta's help page. Moving the tab out is the strongest of the three, because Meta states Muse can act only inside its own tab group.

Q: Does Muse ask before it spends money?

A: The Web Store listing says Muse will ask you before it does anything that may cost you money when it is using a service on your behalf, and that you can approve actions before they happen and review what Muse sends and spends. Approval defaults are managed by the Web access default setting in Permissions under Settings, per the help page.

Q: Can I see what Muse did in my browser?

A: The listing says Muse keeps a full activity log so you can review every action it takes, and that you can revoke any connector at any time. The help page also warns that Muse's activity may show up in your Google account and browsing history.

Q: Will using the extension change the ads I see?

A: It can, on Meta's own statement. The help page says Muse's activity may affect the ads, recommendations, and content you see on and off Meta Company Products.

Q: Has an independent outlet tested whether the extension is safe?

A: No tested safety verdict was found on November 20, 2026. DuckDuckGo searches found no CNET or Verge article on the extension's safety dated November 19 or 20, and the Verge Muse hub read that day was last updated November 18 with no extension entry. Digital Trends covered the launch in full on November 19 and gave no tested verdict. This site rechecks on November 27, 2026.
