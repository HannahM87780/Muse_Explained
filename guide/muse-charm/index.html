Muse Charm explained: Meta's keychain AI gadget

No price, no firm ship date, and a hardware team still finalizing the insides. What Meta has actually said about the Tamagotchi-sized gadget that puts Muse on your keychain, and the three numbers that will decide whether it is worth buying.

By Marvin Jones

Last checked against the live product: October 10, 2026.

At the end of his Meta Connect keynote on September 23, Mark Zuckerberg closed with a "one more thing": a keychain-sized gadget called Muse Charm. About two inches of touchscreen, a fingerprint sensor, its own 5G connection, and a cartoon avatar named Jolly on the display. Press the sensor, talk, and Muse answers without you touching your phone.

That is the pitch. The reality, over two weeks later: Meta has announced no price, no firm ship date beyond a December holiday target, and its own hardware team said onstage the components are still being finalized. Only a handful of units exist. Here is what is confirmed, what is rumor, and the questions Meta still owes answers to.

What the Charm is

The Charm is a pocket-sized accessory for the Muse agent, not a second agent and not a phone replacement. It is about the size of an AirPods case, with a translucent shell you can see the circuitry through, and it hangs off a keychain or a wrist on a lanyard. The screen shows Jolly, Meta's customizable Muse avatar (launch coverage lists a mascot, a mango, a labradoodle, and a piece of toast in sunglasses among the options). You set the avatar's look, voice, and outfits in the Muse app on iPhone or Android, which is also how the Charm gets configured. The agent itself runs in the cloud.

The interaction is deliberately minimal: press the fingerprint sensor and talk. Meta's real-time voice model answers on the spot instead of pausing to process. Zuckerberg's claim for it: "packed the whole Muse experience, including the whole real-time voice and avatar stack, into something that fits on a keychain and is always available to talk to." For people not wearing Meta's smart glasses, he pitched it as the fastest way to talk to Muse and show it what is around you.

It carries its own 5G connection (plus Wi-Fi, per some reports), so it works away from your phone. It cannot make phone or video calls. It plays music and video, has a speaker, microphones, and USB-C charging. One odd detail from the launch coverage: two Charms can recognize and interact with each other when their owners are nearby.

And once you own it, using it reportedly costs nothing extra: no paid plan required, just the free weekly usage allowance. That is from third-party reporting, not from Meta, which has published no pricing page for the Charm at all.

The design comes from Meta's newer design studio, led by Alan Dye, the former Apple interface design chief, working with his deputy Billy Sorrentino. Per reports, the project was compressed from a 2027 plan into about nine months because Zuckerberg wanted hardware to show this year.

The camera row is the one to watch: most launch coverage lists front and rear cameras for giving Muse visual context, but at least one outlet reports none. Meta has not published a spec sheet to settle it.

The price row is the whole story right now. The only number in circulation is a rumor-grade $129 to $149 from a single-source spec leak, plus one report that it will be priced "similarly to a smartwatch." Treat both as guesses. Meta's official line remains "more details later this year."

Caution

Anything you read about the Charm's price, battery life, or camera setup that does not come from Meta is speculation. The hardware is not finished: Meta's own team said the component layout was still being finalized at the reveal, and only a few units have been built. Reporting based on the keynote is reporting on a prototype.

Why the gadget graveyard matters, and why this one might differ

Every AI gadget before this one failed, and the scorecard is ugly. The Humane AI Pin launched at $699 plus $24 a month in 2024, sold fewer than 10,000 units against a 100,000 target, and every pin stopped working in February 2025 after HP bought the company's assets. The $199 Rabbit R1 sold about 130,000 units off a viral debut, but by September 2024 only around 5,000 people used it daily. The Friend pendant drew graffiti-covered subway ads and eye-rolls. Meta itself bought the Limitless pendant company in December 2025 and pulled that pendant from sale.

The honest objection, from The Register: what does a separate gadget do that the app cannot? Meta has not really answered that yet.

But the order of operations is different this time, and it matters. Humane and Rabbit sold hardware hoping to find users for their assistants. Meta already has the hit: Muse topped the app store charts in September with millions of downloads in its first weeks. The Charm is not asking anyone to try a new assistant, only a new way of reaching one they may already use. And if the Charm flops, Muse keeps going on phones, Mac, and glasses. The gadget is optional in a way the AI Pin never was.

The strategy read is worth naming too. One analyst called Connect 2026 "not primarily a hardware event, it was a permission event": every device converts an announcement into a new rail the assistant is authorized to run on. A dedicated device also reduces Meta's dependence on Apple's and Google's app stores for reaching Muse users. And the open competitive question is OpenAI, which reportedly has its own consumer hardware effort expected before the end of 2026. Meta won the announcement race; the sales race has not started.

The age-gate problem nobody has solved

Here is the sharpest criticism of the Charm, and it has nothing to do with specs. The Muse app has an 18+ age gate at signup. A gadget in a gift box does not. Memeburn's formulation: "An app has an age gate at signup. A gadget in a gift box doesn't." The person unwrapping it in December may be 12, not 32.

The fingerprint sensor locks the device to one user, but it cannot tell how old that user is. Meta has not explained how setup-time age checks would work on a device whose whole sales model is the collectible craze: Pop Mart, the company behind Labubu, reported $2.55 billion in first-half 2026 revenue with the Labubu line alone at $662 million. Gifting is the business model, and gifts go to kids.

Child-safety advocates have noticed. One widely shared post put it bluntly: a camera and microphone wrapped in a character a toddler wants to hug, officially 18+, with every consent control assuming an adult who reads permission screens. Meta's response so far is silence on the setup question.

The privacy question

A keychain gadget with cameras, microphones, its own cellular connection, and a fingerprint sensor, from the company behind the last decade of privacy settlements, was always going to get a reaction. The internet reached for the same joke within hours: "evil Tamagotchi." One survey found opinion split almost evenly: about 37 percent "not to taste," 36 percent "innovative," 27 percent "like some." The split reads as trust, not hardware.

Meta's answer is the confidential-computing architecture: the agent runs in a Secure VM, and Meta hired Signal founder Moxie Marlinspike to lead the confidential VM work. The critique, also widely shared: that seals your data, but not Meta's choices. The model's objectives, how it ranks options, and which partners it routes you to (Walmart, Best Buy, Sephora, Instacart, Expedia, PayPal) stay Meta's. Your data is sealed. Your choices are not.

Field note

The Charm's privacy story is the same as the phone app's privacy story in a smaller package. Everything in the trust guides applies: check what the agent can see, connector by connector, and assume anything the cameras capture transits Meta's infrastructure until Meta documents otherwise. The device being cute does not change the permission model. My read is that the cuteness is designed to make you forget the permission model, which is exactly the point the critics are making.

Who it is actually for

Memeburn asked this head-on and the answer is narrow: people who want to use their phone less and hand off errands by voice, provided the price and data costs stay low. A camera you point deliberately is less intrusive than one on your face, which is a genuine advantage over glasses for the privacy-conscious. But until Meta publishes three numbers, price, battery life, and the cost of the 5G connection, plus an explanation of what the cameras store, nobody can responsibly recommend it. If the price lands near earbuds and no separate data plan is required, the phone-light user is a realistic buyer. If not, it is a novelty for early adopters. I would not pre-order on a target date from a team still finalizing components; I would wait for the three numbers.

For context on the hardware moment: the camera-free Ray-Ban Meta Audio glasses ship October 13 at $349, the first Meta hardware in this wave with a real price and a real date. The Charm is the next test of whether Meta can do it twice.

Quick answers

What is the Muse Charm?

A keychain-sized device Meta unveiled at Connect 2026 that gives you voice access to the Muse agent without your phone. Two-inch touchscreen, fingerprint sensor, its own 5G connection, customizable Jolly avatar.

How much does it cost?

Meta has not announced a price. The only figures in circulation ($129 to $149 from one leak, "like a smartwatch" from one report) are rumors.

When can I buy it?

Meta is targeting December 2026, in time for the holidays. Its own team said the hardware was still being finalized at the reveal, so treat the date as a target, not a promise.

Does it replace my phone?

No. It is set up through the Muse app on iPhone or Android, the agent runs in the cloud, and it cannot make phone or video calls.

Does it need a subscription?

On current reporting, no: it runs on the free weekly usage allowance. But there is no Meta pricing page for the Charm yet, and nobody knows who pays for the 5G data.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: Does the Charm have cameras?

A: Most launch coverage says front and rear cameras for giving Muse visual context; one outlet reports none. Meta has not published a spec sheet, so treat this as disputed.

Q: Is it safe for kids?

A: Meta rates Muse 18+, but the Charm is gift-box hardware with no app-style signup gate, and Meta has not explained setup-time age checks. The fingerprint sensor verifies identity, not age.

Q: Why would I buy this instead of just using the app?

A: That is the question Meta has not fully answered. The pitch is speed: press and talk, no unlocking a phone or opening an app, with your surroundings visible to the agent. The honest answer is that nobody knows yet whether that convenience is worth a second device to charge and carry.

Q: What happens if the Charm flops like the Humane Pin?

A: Unlike the Pin, the Charm is an accessory to an agent that already runs on phones, Mac, and glasses. If the hardware fails, Muse continues. That is the structural difference from every entry in the gadget graveyard.

Q: Is OpenAI making one too?

A: Reports say OpenAI has a consumer hardware effort expected before the end of 2026, with no confirmed name, price, or date. Meta won the announcement race; the sales race has not started.
