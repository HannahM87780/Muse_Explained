"Free" needs a credit card: how Muse's free tier really works

Muse costs nothing to start, but signup has asked most people for a payment card before the agent does any work. Here is why Meta wants it, what you are actually charged (nothing, until you subscribe), and what really happens when the free allowance runs out.

By Marvin Jones

Last checked against the live product: October 16, 2026.

The strangest screen in Muse's signup is not the permissions list. It is the card field. The product is free, Meta says most people will stay free, and yet the free tier opens with a request normally associated with a checkout. That one screen explains a lot of the suspicion around the launch, and it is worth slowing down for, because the card is doing three different jobs at once and Meta has only clearly explained one of them.

Why a free product wants your card

Start with the explanation Meta gave at launch, as reported by TechCrunch on launch day: Muse is free to use, with subscription plans that kick in as usage increases, which is why Muse requires a payment card to get started. Read that carefully. The card is not payment for the free tier. It is pre-positioning for the paid tiers, so that an upgrade, if you ever choose one, has nothing standing in its way.

The second job is structural. Muse is not a chatbot. It is an agent that can buy things, book things, and commit you to transactions, with your approval, through payment partners like Stripe Link and Shop Pay. An account that may one day move money needs a payment method attached to it in a way an ordinary chat app never does.

The third job is age verification. Muse is 18 and over only, and a payment card is one of the standard proofs of adulthood online. Third-party signup guides describe card verification as one of the age-check routes, alongside connecting a Facebook or Instagram account. Meta has not published a step-by-step account of which check each new user gets, so treat the exact flow as something that can vary by account.

Field note

The sequencing is the problem. The card ask arrives before you have seen Muse do a single useful thing, when trust is at its lowest and the request looks most like a trap. Products usually ask for a card after they have proven value. Muse asks first and explains later, and some of the launch skepticism this site has covered traces straight back to that ordering.

What the card is not: a charge waiting to happen

Here is the part that matters most, and it is better news than the signup screen suggests.

You are not charged for the free tier. There is no published pay-as-you-go overage in Muse, no per-token bill that quietly starts when your allowance runs out. Meta's own subscription help page puts the choice at the limit in plain terms: if you reach your free usage limit and want more, you can upgrade to a paid subscription, or you can wait until your free usage limit refreshes. Upgrade or wait. Those are the two doors, and both of them require a decision from you. The card on file does not open a third door where money leaves on its own.

Purchases are a separate matter, and they stay separate. When Muse buys something for you, that is a transaction you approve, through a payment partner, at a price shown to you. That is commerce, not subscription billing, and it is covered in this site's guide to whether Muse asks before it spends your money.

One confusion to clear up: the hold. Some signup guides describe a small temporary authorization during card-based age verification, about $1 in the United States write-ups. An authorization hold is not a charge; it is a pending check that falls off. Meta has not documented this step on its own pages that I can find, so file it under reported by third-party guides, plausible, and unconfirmed. If you see a tiny pending amount during signup, that is the likely explanation, and if it posts as a real charge, that is worth disputing, because nothing in Meta's published terms describes a signup fee.

How much free is free, exactly?

Less precisely answered than you would hope.

Mark Zuckerberg put a number on it at launch: up to 100 million tokens per week on the free tier. That figure comes from his own posts and launch comments, and it has been repeated by nearly every guide since. But look at Meta's own subscription help page today and the free tier gets no number at all. It says only that Muse is available for free with a usage limit. The numbers Meta publishes there are the paid ones: Power at $20 a month gets 500 million Muse tokens per week, and Maximum at $100 a month gets 3 billion per week.

So the honest summary is this: the free ceiling of roughly 100 million tokens a week is a launch statement from Meta's CEO, widely reported and never contradicted, but it is not a figure Meta currently publishes in its own subscription documentation. Plan around the meter in the app, not around the headline number, because a token is not a task. One long research job with a lot of browsing can cost more than a week of short errands, and Meta offers no conversion table.

And the refresh itself? Meta says the limit refreshes. It has not said, in the help pages I checked, whether that refresh runs on a fixed weekly schedule or a rolling window tied to your usage. If the exact reset mechanics matter to your workflow, that is still an open question, and this page will update when Meta answers it.

Caution

Some recent guides now say no card is needed to start, and they may be right for the accounts they tested. Meta has been adjusting onboarding since launch, and the help pages describe subscription requirements without spelling out a free-tier card rule either way. What the signup flow shows your account, on your device, today, is the ground truth. If your flow never asks for a card, you have lost nothing. If it does ask, the ask matches the launch design described above, not a scam or a billing error.

How to check your usage before it checks you

The meter is the whole game on the free tier, so learn where it lives before you need it.

In the mobile app, open Menu, then Settings. Your usage information sits there, above Manage subscription, which is also where you can review your subscription status and current payment method. On the web at muse.ai, it is Menu in the bottom left, then Settings, then look below Usage for Manage subscription. Meta's help pages also note that a subscription has to be managed from the same platform and account type you subscribed on, so if you ever do upgrade, remember where you did it.

Meta says the app warns you when your free usage is running out and presents subscription options at that point. My advice is simpler: glance at the meter after any day you handed Muse something big, a long research run, an inbox cleanup, a background goal left running. Heavy agent work drains the allowance in bursts, not in a smooth line, and the warning arrives after the burst, not before it.

Should the card stop you?

That depends on what your objection actually is.

If your worry is surprise billing, the published mechanics are on your side: no charge for free use, no overage billing, an upgrade that you have to choose, and purchases that need your approval. The card is a stored payment method, not a standing instruction to spend.

If your worry is handing Meta a payment credential at all, that is a different and legitimate objection, and at launch it amounted to a closed door: no card, no agent. That may be loosening as onboarding changes roll out, but as of this writing Meta has not published a clear no-card path for the standard signup, and this site will not pretend otherwise.

Either way, the free tier is best understood as free with paperwork. The price of admission is not money. It is a card on file, an 18-and-over check, and a usage meter you have to watch yourself. For a lot of people that trade is fine. It just is not the frictionless free the headline promised, and now you know where the friction lives.

Quick answers

Is Muse really free?

Yes, with a weekly usage limit. Meta expects most users to stay on the free tier. Power costs $20 a month and Maximum costs $100 a month, and both mainly buy more usage.

Why does the free tier ask for a credit card?

Per Meta's launch explanation, because subscriptions kick in as usage increases, so the card is on file before you need one. The card can also serve the account's payment and age-verification needs. You are not charged for the free tier itself.

What happens when I hit the free limit?

You choose: upgrade to a paid subscription, or wait until your free usage limit refreshes. Meta's help page offers exactly those two options. There is no published pay-as-you-go overage that bills your card automatically.

How big is the free allowance?

Zuckerberg put it at up to 100 million tokens per week at launch. Meta's current subscription help page publishes no number for the free tier, only the paid ceilings of 500 million tokens a week for Power and 3 billion a week for Maximum. Your in-app usage meter is the number that counts for your account.

Can I use Muse without a card at all?

Unclear, and changing. Launch signup required one. Some newer third-party guides report starting without one. Meta has not published a definitive statement either way, so go by what your own signup flow asks for.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: Is there a signup fee or a charge for adding my card?

A: No fee is described in Meta's published subscription materials. Third-party guides mention a small temporary authorization hold during card-based age verification, around $1. A hold is not a charge and should fall off on its own. Meta has not documented the hold itself, so treat that detail as reported, not confirmed.

Q: Will Muse ever charge my card without asking?

A: For subscriptions, charging starts when you subscribe to Power or Maximum, and those plans renew monthly until cancelled. For purchases Muse makes on your behalf, the design is approval first, through payment partners like Stripe Link. The free tier on its own does not bill you.

Q: Does the free usage limit reset on a fixed day?

A: Meta's help pages say the free usage limit refreshes, without specifying a fixed weekday or a rolling window. That detail is unpublished as of this writing.

Q: If I upgrade, where do I manage or cancel it?

A: In the Muse app under Menu, then Settings, then Manage subscription below your usage information, or on the web at muse.ai under Menu, then Settings, then Manage subscription below Usage. Meta says you must manage a subscription from the same platform and account you used to subscribe.

Q: Is the $20 to $100 pricing the same everywhere?

A: The figures Meta publishes are $20 a month for Power and $100 a month for Maximum. Two outlets have reported lower web prices, $16 and $80, against higher in-app prices, which looks like an app store markup split. That split is reported, not published by Meta, so check the price shown to your account before subscribing.
