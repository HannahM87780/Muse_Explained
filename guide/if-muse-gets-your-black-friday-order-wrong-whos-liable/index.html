If Muse Gets Your Black Friday Order Wrong, Who's Liable? The UK Terms Have No Cap. The Retailer Question Is Still Open

The Guardian on November 27, 2026 asks who is liable if the bot gets an order wrong. Politico Europe, the same day, found one part of an answer in the fine print: the UK Muse terms carry no financial liability cap, while the US terms cap liability at $250, and the CMA says Meta changed the UK terms after weeks of engagement. No retailer in the Guardian report assigns the cost of a wrong order. Sainsbury's own release from November 24 says its usual customer service puts things right if anything goes wrong, with no refund rule attached.

By Marvin Jones

Last checked against the live product: November 27, 2026.

Verdict: partly answered, at the terms level only. Politico Europe, dated November 27, 2026, compared the fine print and reports that the UK Muse terms carry no financial liability cap, while the US terms cap liability at $250, with exceptions Politico lists below. The Competition and Markets Authority told Politico that Meta made changes to the UK terms after weeks of engagement to make them fairer for consumers. The retailer question is not settled. I checked the Guardian report dated November 27, 2026, the Sainsbury's press release dated November 24, 2026, and DuckDuckGo searches on November 27 for Muse terms from M&S, JD Sports, Currys, and Meta. No source in that set assigns the cost of a wrong Muse order to the retailer, to Meta, or to the shopper. You approved the basket: does that make the mistake yours? On this record, nobody has said so, and nobody has said the opposite either. This site rechecks the terms against a primary source on December 5, 2026.

Promise Ledger

This post will be checked again on December 5, 2026, or sooner if a named retailer or Meta publishes terms first, for three rows:

  • Row 1, retailer liability: open. No M&S, Sainsbury's, JD Sports, or Currys page found on November 27, 2026 states who pays for a wrong Muse order. Sainsbury's release says its usual customer service puts things right if anything goes wrong, with no refund rule and no cost assignment attached.
  • Row 2, Meta liability: partly answered by a secondary source, not yet verified against a primary. Politico Europe, November 27, 2026, reports that the UK terms carry no financial cap and that the US terms cap liability at $250, and quotes the CMA saying Meta changed the UK terms after weeks of engagement. Meta's shopping page says Muse works through merchant support flows on returns and disputes, with your approval. It does not state that Meta bears any cost. The Muse terms page returned an error page when opened on November 27, so this site has not verified the terms wording from the terms themselves. The December 5 recheck tries the primary terms again.
  • Row 3, a named wrong order: open for the UK, with a named US cancellation now on the record. No named shopper, wrong item, or completed refund for a UK Muse order appears in the Guardian report. CNBC, November 27, 2026, reports a named US case in a different category: Maia Bittner gave Muse the go ahead to buy windshield wipers from AutoZone, and about 17 hours later Muse told her AutoZone had canceled the order for an unspecified error. A cancellation is not a wrong item delivered, and a US case is not a UK rule. Both limits are printed where the case appears below.

Each row closes only on a named source: a retailer or Meta page that assigns the cost in words, or a named shopper report with the item, the total, and the outcome.

What the Guardian actually reports

The Guardian, by Sarah Butler, dated Friday November 27, 2026 at 02:00 EST, which is 01:00 CST, frames the UK Black Friday test around one question in its headline: if the bot gets it wrong, who is liable? The article reports Muse launching in the UK this week, with Meta teamed with Marks & Spencer, Sainsbury's, JD Sports, and Currys.

The liability answer in the article is that there is no answer yet. The two liability lines both come from Mark Howell, director of European retail at Rithum, an e-commerce firm. Howell says retailers in Europe are asking who is liable if the bot gets it wrong, and that retailers will not be comfortable handing checkouts entirely to bots until responsibility and liability questions are resolved. No retailer executive, no Meta statement, and no shopper in the article accepts or assigns liability for a wrong order. No refund rule for a Muse order appears in the piece.

That is the finding, and I am printing it as a finding rather than filling the gap with the approval step. Approval and liability are different questions. The Guardian settles the first one and leaves the second one open.

The approval rule, and what it does not decide

In the UK, Muse must ask for your approval before making a purchase or sending an email, per the Guardian. A separate security agent checks the actions against the permissions you have granted, per the same report. Meta's own UK post dated November 23, 2026, banked on this site on November 24, states the same rule in Meta's words: Muse will ask for your permission before it sends an email or makes a purchase.

Meta also states a limit on what Muse sees. The Guardian reports Meta saying Muse had no visibility into people's passwords or payment methods, and did not share users' conversations with advertisers. Meta's UK post says Muse will not ask for your passwords, bank account details, or payment information, and that you enter those yourself in the secure checkout.

Hold those two facts at their actual size. An approval tap means you saw a basket or a total before the order went through, if the flow worked as described. It does not, on any terms read for this post, transfer the cost of a wrong item to you. It also does not protect you if the error is in the basket itself: the wrong size, the wrong dietary swap, the duplicate item you approved without noticing. No source read on November 27 separates an error you approved from an error Muse introduced after approval. That separation is the rule a shopper needs, and it has not been published.

Caution

Do not treat an approval screen as a liability waiver, and do not treat it as insurance either. On the November 27 record it is neither. If a Muse order arrives wrong this weekend, keep the confirmation, the basket you approved, and the total. Any retailer or Meta support flow will ask for those three items first. No published rule in this post guarantees how the dispute ends.

The Sainsbury's flow: tell Muse about your week

Sainsbury's is the only one of the four Guardian partners with a worked example in the piece. Sainsbury's suggests customers can tell Muse about their week, and the agent will help build a shopping list for dinner parties, different dietary requirements, or suggested menus and recipes, per the Guardian.

Mark Given, chief technology and marketing officer at Sainsbury's, is quoted in the Guardian saying the retailer was starting carefully and listening to customers, and that AI can take on routine tasks such as checking offers and building the basket. Sainsbury's own press release is dated Tuesday November 24, 2026. It was read for this post through the November 27 research. The release gives his title as Chief Technology, Marketing and Data Officer. It also adds mechanics the Guardian does not. Muse shops the Sainsbury's website and Tu.co.uk as a customer would, seeing the same products, prices, and offers, and can consider Aldi Price Match and Nectar Prices.

The release also carries the closest sentence to a liability rule in this whole post. Sainsbury's says an order through Muse is a Sainsbury's order like any other, colleagues pick and deliver it, and Sainsbury's usual customer service puts things right if anything goes wrong. Read that sentence closely. It promises a service channel. It does not promise a refund, it does not name who pays for a wrong item Muse chose, and it does not mention Meta at all. The same release says customers can choose to see their basket and total before anything is ordered, every order goes through Sainsbury's own checkout with the usual confirmation and a chance to amend or cancel, and customers can stop using or disconnect Muse at any time.

Sainsbury's general refunds and returns help pages exist, and the November 27 searches surfaced them. Their listed topics are general, with no Muse mention in the topics shown. A general returns policy covers a change of mind or a faulty item in the ordinary way. It is not a Muse wrong order rule, and this post does not present it as one.

The other three partners: named, not quoted

M&S, JD Sports, and Currys appear in the Guardian as launch partners. No executive from any of the three is quoted in the article. DuckDuckGo site searches on November 27 found no Muse shopping page on marksandspencer.com, no Meta Muse page on jdsports.com, and no Muse shopping assistant page on currys.co.uk. The JD Sports results were for Nike products with Muse in the product name, a different Muse. The Currys results were for Meta AI glasses and general pages.

So the liability record for three of the four named partners is empty, not negative. There is no statement to disagree with. A shopper buying through Muse at any of the three this weekend has, on this record, the retailer's ordinary terms and nothing Muse specific.

The partner list problem, stated plainly

The Guardian names four partners in this article: Marks & Spencer, Sainsbury's, JD Sports, and Currys. Meta's UK post dated November 23, 2026 names 15: Marks & Spencer, Debenhams Group, Currys, Ocado Retail, Trainline, Argos, Sainsbury's, ASOS, Jet2, LOOKFANTASTIC, MyProtein, Cult Beauty, JustPark, easyJet, and JD Sports. This site counted those 15 names in Meta's paragraph on November 24.

Both lists can be accurate at once if the Guardian is naming the Black Friday shopping names it chose to feature. Neither source says that. I am printing the two lists side by side because a reader comparing coverage this weekend will see four names in one place and 15 in another, and the difference is in the sources, not in this site's counting.

What Meta's own shopping page adds

Meta's page titled Muse for Shopping, at ai.meta.com, with no date shown, was read in the November 27 research. It says Muse finds, buys, tracks, and returns for you, that you approve purchases before they happen, and that a one time card number is generated at checkout so your real card stays hidden from the merchant and your agent. On returns it says Muse works through merchant support flows, files returns and disputes, and follows up until the matter is resolved, all with your approval.

That paragraph describes a helper for the dispute process. It assigns no cost to Meta, sets no deadline, and contains no UK specific shopping terms. The Muse terms page that search listed first returned an error page when opened on November 27, and the Meta AI terms page did the same, so no liability wording from either document is quoted or summarized in this post. An error page is not evidence about what the terms say. It is evidence that this check could not read them, which is why the December 5 recheck exists.

The demand numbers, with their real owners

Two figures from the Guardian piece need their correct owners, because early summaries on this site grouped them under one firm.

YouGov is the source for the concern figure: almost half of shoppers using AI, 48 percent, are concerned about biased or sponsored recommendations, per the Guardian citing YouGov. YouGov also says a third of people planning to shop during Black Friday intend to get help from an AI agent or tool in some way, up from 19 percent last year, per the Guardian.

KPMG is the source for the use figure: just 12 percent of those planning to shop during Black Friday intended to use an AI tool specifically to find deals, slightly lower than the 15 percent using it last year, per the Guardian citing KPMG research. Linda Ellett, head of consumer, retail and leisure at KPMG, is quoted in the piece saying trust would remain an important factor in whether people used AI agents, and that shoppers would be reluctant to share personal data with companies they did not trust.

Neither figure is a Muse figure. The 48 percent covers shoppers using AI generally, and the 12 percent names tools such as ChatGPT or Gemini in the Guardian's wording. Neither figure is a Black Friday result. Both were published on Black Friday morning, before the day's orders existed. Kantar, in the same passage of the Guardian piece, found only 23 percent trusted chatbot recommendations. Three firms, three different questions, and no basis for adding them together.

Field note

The gap between the YouGov third who intend to get AI help in some way and the KPMG 12 percent who intend to use an AI tool to find deals is the honest shape of this Black Friday. Help is a wide word. It covers asking for gift ideas and handing over the checkout, and only the second one creates a wrong order to argue about. The liability question in this post belongs to the smaller group, and that group is the one with no published rule.

Two other voices in the piece, at their actual weight

Sharon Iles, senior apparel analyst at GlobalData, raises a returns point in the Guardian: poor product information and fit or sizing problems could lead to higher return rates when shoppers search in natural language. That is a forecast about returns volume, not a statement about who pays for a return.

John Roberts, founder and chief executive of AO.com, is quoted on positioning his company to be the best option for whichever AI agent a shopper uses, and on agents taking an advertising cut in future. AO is not one of the four named Muse partners in the article. His remarks describe a retailer preparing for agents generally. They do not set AO's Muse terms, because no AO Muse flow is described in the piece.

The Guardian also notes, as context, that Amazon has sued Perplexity over a shopping feature that automates placing orders. A lawsuit between two other companies is not a liability rule for Muse orders, and this post uses it only to show that the fight over agent checkout is already in court elsewhere.

What Politico found in the fine print

Politico Europe, by Joseph Bambridge, dated November 27, 2026 at 12:25 PM CET, which is 05:25 AM CST, compared the US and UK Muse terms directly. Its headline is that Meta's UK rollout for Muse exposes it to more liability, and its deck says the UK terms give consumers far more protection than the US terms.

Politico reports three differences. First, the US terms cap liability at $250, except for claims arising from Meta's gross negligence, willful misconduct, or fraud. Second, the US terms absolve Meta, to the fullest extent permitted by applicable law, for losses caused by Muse taking an unintended, erroneous, or unauthorized action. Third, the UK terms carry no financial cap. In the UK, a narrower set of exceptions applies only where Meta has acted with reasonable skill and care or professional diligence. Politico notes that standard is implied into business to consumer service contracts under UK consumer law.

The regulator is quoted, not paraphrased at a distance. The Competition and Markets Authority, which enforces consumer law in the UK, told Politico that Meta had made changes to Muse's UK terms following weeks of engagement, to make them fairer for consumers and to reflect UK consumer law. The CMA also said it expects Muse to be the first of many personal AI agents in the UK, and invited users to report issues. Meta declined to comment on detailed regulatory discussions, per Politico. Politico reports that Meta says Muse can lend a helping hand and publicly acknowledges the tool can and will still make mistakes.

Read that finding at its actual size. It is a terms comparison by one outlet, read in full on November 27. Politico's text, as read, quoted the CMA directly and reported Meta's position. It did not link to a CMA or Meta primary document in the text read for this post. This site's own attempt to read the Muse terms on November 27 returned an error page. So the no cap finding is reported here as Politico's finding, with the CMA quoted inside it. It is not presented as wording this site verified from the terms themselves. The December 5 recheck exists for that verification, as well as for any retailer rule.

A liability cap is also not a refund rule for a wrong grocery item. A cap limits what Meta can be liable for under its terms. It does not tell a Sainsbury's shopper who pays for the wrong size or the wrong swap in a basket the shopper approved. Both questions belong in this post. Only the first one moved today.

The first named cancellation, from the United States

CNBC, by Annie Palmer, published November 27, 2026 at 10:27 AM EST and updated at 11:43 AM EST, reports a named case that is not a wrong item case. Maia Bittner, chief product officer at the fintech startup Imprint, needed windshield wipers for her car last month. She gave Muse the go ahead to buy them from AutoZone. About 17 hours later, Muse notified her that AutoZone had canceled the order due to an unspecified error, per CNBC. The error is printed here as unspecified because CNBC does not name a cause.

CNBC reports what Muse advised next, from a screenshot of the message: the most reliable path was for Bittner to order on Amazon herself. Amazon blocked Muse from shopping on its platform in September, per CNBC, saying it never agreed to participate. That advice is a fact about the fallback Muse offered in this case. It is not a UK fact, and it does not settle who bore any cost in the AutoZone case. CNBC does not report a refund dispute in the Bittner account as read.

CNBC puts the case in a wider split. Walmart and Sephora have opened their sites to agents, per CNBC. Amazon, eBay, United Airlines, and Kohl's limit what agents can do or block them outright. Coach and Gap allow bots to browse but not check out, per an August audit by Billions Network cited by CNBC. Two figures in the same article carry their own owners. Bain and Co. research puts agentic purchases at 0.4 percent of global e-commerce transactions and 0.2 percent of value. A study published earlier this month, cited by CNBC without naming Muse as one of the models tested in the text read, instructed 22 models from 12 AI labs to buy a TV, a laptop, and a hair appliance. The agents failed about 43 percent of test runs.

Records this post does not change

The United States record stands apart from this UK test, with one contrast now explicit. Politico reports that the US Muse terms cap liability at $250, subject to the exceptions listed above, while the UK terms carry no financial cap. Walmart through Muse charges a card saved at Walmart, with a per order limit set in an Agent Profile, per Walmart's own pages read on November 26, 2026. None of those Walmart controls is a UK rule, and none is imported into the Sainsbury's record here. Consumer pricing still stands at free, with Power at $20 a month and Maximum at $100 a month, and no price in pounds has been published by Meta in the sources read for this site's UK coverage. The Amazon talks reported November 25, 2026 produced no deal and no checkout. None of those records answers the UK liability question, and this post does not borrow from any of them to fill it.

Quick answers

Who is liable if Muse gets my order wrong in the UK?

Partly answered at the terms level, not settled at the retailer level. Politico Europe, November 27, 2026, reports that the UK Muse terms carry no financial liability cap, while the US terms cap liability at $250, and quotes the CMA saying Meta changed the UK terms after weeks of engagement. The Guardian poses the retailer question through Mark Howell at Rithum and reports no answer from any retailer. No retailer terms found on November 27 assign the cost of a wrong order to the retailer, to Meta, or to you.

Does approving the basket make the mistake mine?

No source read on November 27, 2026 says that. UK Muse must ask your approval before a purchase, per the Guardian and per Meta's UK post dated November 23, 2026. Approval is a control step. No published rule in this check turns it into a transfer of liability.

What does Sainsbury's say if something goes wrong?

Sainsbury's press release dated November 24, 2026 says an order through Muse is a Sainsbury's order like any other, and its usual customer service puts things right if anything goes wrong. The release gives no refund rule, no cost assignment, and no mention of Meta paying anything.

Can I see the basket before Sainsbury's orders it?

Yes, per the same Sainsbury's release. Customers can choose to see their basket and total before anything is ordered, every order goes through Sainsbury's own checkout, and there is a chance to amend or cancel.

Does Muse see my password or payment method in the UK?

Meta says no, in both records. The Guardian reports Meta saying Muse had no visibility into people's passwords or payment methods. Meta's UK post says you enter payment information yourself in the secure checkout.

What do the 48 percent and 12 percent figures mean?

YouGov: 48 percent of shoppers using AI are concerned about biased or sponsored recommendations, per the Guardian. KPMG: 12 percent of people planning to shop during Black Friday intended to use an AI tool specifically to find deals, per the Guardian. Neither figure is Muse specific, and neither is a result from Black Friday itself.

Which retailers does the Guardian name?

Marks & Spencer, Sainsbury's, JD Sports, and Currys. Meta's UK post dated November 23, 2026 names 15 partners. This post prints both lists without merging them.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: Did any retailer publish Muse shopping terms before Black Friday?

A: None was found on November 27, 2026. DuckDuckGo searches that day found Sainsbury's launch release and its general help pages, and no Muse page at all for M&S, JD Sports, or Currys. Sainsbury's general refunds topics show no Muse mention in the topics listed.

Q: What does Meta's shopping page say about returns?

A: It says Muse works through merchant support flows, files returns and disputes, and follows up until the matter is resolved, all with your approval, per the page read on November 27, 2026. It does not say Meta pays for a wrong order.

Q: Could the Muse terms settle this?

A: In part, a comparison of them already has. Politico Europe read the US and UK terms and reported on November 27, 2026 that the UK terms carry no financial cap and the US terms cap liability at $250. This site could not read the terms itself. The Muse terms page and the Meta AI terms page both returned error pages when opened on November 27, 2026. No wording from either document is quoted directly in this post, and the December 5 recheck will try the primary terms again.

Q: Is the US Walmart approval step the same rule?

A: The flows rhyme and the records are separate. Walmart's help page, read November 26, 2026, sets a per order limit in an Agent Profile and requires approval above that limit. The UK record in this post has an approval rule and no published per order limit. Do not carry one retailer's control into another retailer's checkout.

Q: Has a wrong Muse order been reported yet?

A: No named UK wrong item case appears in the Guardian report. A named US cancellation does appear in CNBC on November 27, 2026: Maia Bittner's AutoZone windshield wiper order, canceled about 17 hours later for an unspecified error. A cancellation is not a wrong item delivered. No shopper anecdote about a wrong item, a refund, or a return is in the Guardian piece. The December 5 check looks for the first named UK wrong item case, a primary copy of the terms, and any retailer rule.

Q: When does this get checked again?

A: December 5, 2026, or sooner if a named retailer or Meta publishes who bears a wrong Muse order. If the answer is still not settled on December 5, this post updates to say so on that date.
